# What a Thrown-Away Tool Can Tell Us

<!-- STEP id="line" -->
## The First Toolmakers

The earliest known stone tools were made about 3.3 million years ago. Your textbook calls this the beginning of "human behaviour", because no other animal makes stone tools of this kind.

This is why humans came to be known as **hominins**, or toolmakers. The word covers modern humans and our early human-like ancestors.

<!-- STEP id="limbs" -->
## Tools as Extra Limbs

A sharp edge lets a hand do new work. Such tools are sometimes called *extra-corporal limbs*, which means limbs outside the body, because they can cut, dig and crack what bare fingers cannot.

<!-- STEP id="oldworld" -->
## The Old World

The earliest human settlements were in Africa, Asia and Europe. Together these three continents are called the Old World.

<!-- STEP id="discard" -->
## Used, Worn Out, Dropped

Now follow one tool. It was made and used until its edge wore out, and then it was dropped and left on the ground.

<!-- STEP id="buried" -->
## The Ground Rises Over It

You might think the tool sinks, but it stays where it fell. Season after season, new earth settles on top of it: dust, flood silt, ash, fallen rock. So the surface rises, and the tool ends up deep below it.

<!-- STEP id="found" -->
## Found Again

Long afterwards, an archaeologist cuts a trench down through those layers and reaches the tool. This is the same kind of trench you saw in Lesson 1, seen this time from the tool's side.

**A worn-out tool left in the ground is evidence of what someone once did there.**

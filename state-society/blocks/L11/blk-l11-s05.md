# Roads and Ports

## What the state did for trade

Well-structured states provided the conditions for trade to flourish. The *Arthaśhāstra* notes that Magadha traded textiles, gems, coral, pearls, metals and minerals across northern, central and southern India.

The state also regulated salt production, kept trade routes safe, and took measures to prevent the adulteration of goods. Adulteration means mixing cheaper material into a product.

<!-- STEP -->
## Two land routes

By around the 6th century BCE, two major land routes had emerged. The **Dakṣhiṇāpatha** is lit. Tap **Uttarāpatha** to see the second route and its cities.

Successive dynasties maintained and expanded these routes, and they linked different parts of the subcontinent.

<!-- STEP -->
## Ports and the sea

**Ports** is lit. Trade links with Mesopotamia already existed in Harappan times. Later, major ports such as **Muziris**, **Kāveripaṭṭinam**, **Arikameḍu** and **Masulipaṭnam** attracted both Indian and foreign traders.

By the early centuries CE, India's trade with Rome had expanded significantly, by sea and overland through Central Asia.

## Goods, people and ideas

The routes moved goods, people and ideas. They connected inland regions with coastal ports, so overland and sea trade worked together.

The *Silappadikāram* mentions two more routes in the Tamil region: Kanchipuram to Poompuhar (Kaveripattinam), and Kanchipuram to Kanyakumari.

# The Rosetta Stone

<!-- STEP id="stone" -->
## Three Scripts, One Message

This was the Rosetta Stone. People could still read Greek, and the three parts carry the same message. So the Greek part worked like a two-language dictionary for the lost script. It was the Greek that mattered: many other stones had survived, and the stone gives no lessons in grammar.

<!-- STEP id="name" -->
## Start With a Name

A dictionary still has to be used, so scholars began with names. A name sounds much the same in any language. The Greek part names a king, Ptolemy, and in the same place the Egyptian part has signs inside an oval ring.

<!-- STEP id="sounds" -->
## One Sign, One Sound

If those signs spell Ptolemy, then each sign may stand for a sound: P, T, O, L, M, Y, S. Scholars then tested those sounds on other names.

<!-- STEP id="decipher" -->
## Read Again

In 1822, the French scholar Jean-Francois Champollion finally deciphered the script, more than 20 years after the stone was found.

**The lost script was read again because it stood beside a language people still knew.** The Sindhu lipi has no such stone yet.

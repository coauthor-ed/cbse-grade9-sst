# Same Day, Two Ends of the Country

<!-- STEP -->
## What the 1 June line actually connects

Look at where that line sits. It is not a line across the middle of India.

It runs along the **southern tip** of the mainland, and separately it runs into the **north-east** - Assam and Meghalaya.

Draw the arc from the Bay of Bengal to each of those two places and the shape is the same both times: **the wind arrives straight off open water.**

- Kerala coast, facing the open Bay.
- Assam and Meghalaya, also facing the Bay across open water.

Both are reached on day one, because both are **coast**.

<!-- STEP -->
## The places that wait

Now find the ones the first line does *not* touch.

Delhi, Punjab, Himachal Pradesh, and the far north-west. These are inland, or they are a long way from any water the wind has crossed.

The wind reaches them **from the side**, over land it has already been over. That takes weeks, and that is what the later lines on the map are recording.

<!-- STEP -->
## So what decides the date

Two candidate explanations, and only one of them survives.

**Heat:** the Thar is the hottest part of the country in May, so the wind should reach it first.

That fails immediately, because the two places on 1 June are not the two hottest places in India in May - and because the Thar, the hottest of all, is the **last** line on the map.

**Facing open water:** what decides it is whether the leading edge can arrive straight off the sea.

**Coast first and together. Inland behind them. Desert last.** Heat is what makes the wind blow at all. It is not what puts it on a particular date.

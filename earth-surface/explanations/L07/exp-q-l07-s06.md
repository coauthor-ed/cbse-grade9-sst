## Reading the Survey, Feature by Feature

The question is really three questions, and taking them in order is the whole of it.

### 1. The valley itself

A **wide flat floor** with **steep straight walls** above it is the shape the ice leaves. A river cutting the same rock leaves sloping sides and a floor that narrows as it climbs.

Nothing about a flood does either of those things. Floods widen and shallow; they do not flatten a floor and stand walls up.

**The valley is glacial.**

### 2. The side valley that ends in mid-air

A tributary valley whose floor stops well above the main floor is a **hanging valley**, and the reason is that the two glaciers did not match.

The main glacier was bigger and had more ice, so it cut its floor down further and deeper. The small glacier joined it partway through, and by the time the ice was gone the small glacier's valley was stranded above the main one.

The thin fall at its end is the visible measure of how much further down the big ice went.

**The fall is not the glacier. It is the record of the difference between two glaciers.**

### 3. The wall holding the lake

An unconsolidated rubble wall across a valley on a slope is a **terminal moraine**: the heap a glacier dropped when it stopped melting at that point.

Unconsolidated means the pieces are not bonded - no cement, no compaction into rock, just rubble sitting at the angle rubble sits at when nothing is holding it up.

**The lake behind it is held up by friction and nothing else.**

### Putting them together

Two of the three features are therefore ice's work on rock - the U-shaped valley and the hanging valley - and both are defined by what the ice *removed*.

The third is different in kind. It is not a shape cut into the rock at all; it is a heap of material *put down*, and the water above it is balanced on it.

Which is exactly why it is the least stable part of the survey. Adding weight, adding water, or shaking it from above removes the only support it has.

# What the Chart Shows

<!-- STEP id="verdict" -->
## Several Beginnings

Farming began separately in several regions, and then it spread outward from each one. The evidence is a chart from your textbook, Fig 4.14, redrawn here with one row for each region.

**Farming did not begin at the same time everywhere.**

<!-- STEP id="read" -->
## How to Read a Row

Time runs from left to right, from 15,000 years ago to today. Take West Asia as the example. The dotted line shows early traces of cultivation, from about 12,500 years ago. The light bar shows cultivation, which means growing plants on purpose, and the dark bar shows domesticated crops, from about 9,500 years ago.

<!-- STEP id="gap" -->
## Far Apart in Time

Now compare where each row starts:

- West Asia, wheat and barley: about 12,500 years ago
- North China, millets: about 10,500
- South China, rice: about 9,500
- Ganga plains, rice: about 7,000
- India, millets: about 5,000
- West Africa, pearl millet: about 4,500

From the first row to the last is about 8,000 years.

<!-- STEP id="plants" -->
## Why Several Beginnings?

You may think that West Asia came first and taught everybody else. That was a fair guess, and the dates alone cannot rule it out, because a slow spread can also take thousands of years.

The better clue is the crops. Rice cannot be a late copy of wheat, because it is a different plant. Historians think that each region tamed plants that grew wild nearby.

**Different local plants make separate beginnings likely, though not certain.**

<!-- STEP id="spread" -->
## Farming Also Spread

Separate beginnings do not mean that nothing travelled. Farming then spread out from each region. At Mehrgarh, about 9,000 years ago, people grew wheat and barley, the same pair as West Asia, so these crops probably reached Mehrgarh from there.

The India row on this chart shows millets. Mehrgarh grew wheat and barley and is older, which is why two Indian dates differ.

<!-- STEP id="order" -->
## The Order Was Different Everywhere

You might expect one fixed order: people farm first, then settle, then make pots. The chart says no. It marks five signs in each row: pottery, cultivation, livestock (farm animals), sedentism (settling in one place) and an agricultural landscape (land shaped by fields and farm work). Now the rows are lined up by what came first, second and third.

- West Asia: settling came first and pottery last.
- The other five regions: pottery came first.
- West Africa: livestock came before cultivation.

**People did not all farm first and settle later.**

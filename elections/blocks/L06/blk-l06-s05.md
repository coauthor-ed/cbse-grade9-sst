# Leaving a Party

<!-- STEP -->
## Many Parties, Free to Join

India has a **multi-party system**: many political parties exist and compete in elections. This reflects the country's rich diversity of languages, cultures, religions and regions.

With so many parties, can someone simply leave one and join another whenever they wish? Yes, they can.

<!-- STEP -->
## When Leaving Is Defection

Things change once a person has been elected on a particular party's ticket. A **ticket** is a party's permission to contest in its name.

**Defection** means abandoning or switching from the party under whose banner the candidate was elected. It may mean joining another party. It may also mean acting independently of the party's decisions, particularly when voting in the legislature.

<!-- STEP -->
## Two Ways to See It

Seen through party discipline and political stability, such actions are considered **political opportunism**: acting for personal gain.

Defection can also be viewed as a way to uphold conscience, or to respond to changing public expectations.

## The Anti-Defection Law

This law was passed in **1985** through the **52nd Constitutional Amendment Act**. Its purpose is to prevent defection, and so give stability to the elected government.

A member of Parliament or a State Assembly can be disqualified from the House for either of two things:

- voluntarily giving up party membership
- voting against the party's instructions, called the **whip**

**The Speaker or Chairman of the House decides what needs to be done.**

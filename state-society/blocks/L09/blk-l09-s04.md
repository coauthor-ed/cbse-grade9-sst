# The Smallest Unit

<!-- STEP -->
## One house

Think of the people you eat with every day. In Vedic society that group was the **kula**, the family, and it was the smallest and most basic social unit.

A kula did not stand alone. It was connected to larger groups around it, and we will add those groups one at a time.

<!-- STEP -->
## Village and group of villages

Several families together formed a village, the **grāma**. A group of grāmas formed a **viśha**, and the viśha had its own head, the **viśhapati**.

So one person already belonged to three groups, each inside the next: the kula, the grāma and the viśha.

<!-- STEP -->
## The people and their protector

Above the viśha was the **jana**, the people, and the **rājā** served as the chief protector of the jana.

Every circle holds the one before it, so a person's family was part of the whole people.

**The family, or kula, was inseparable from the jana.**

<!-- STEP -->
## A household of kin

Words can change as a society changes. During the later Vedic period, **kula** came to mean a household of several members bound by kinship.

Look at the house now. It holds more people than the family we began with, and all of them are related.

<!-- STEP -->
## The father's line

A **gotra** was a patrilineal lineage or clan, traced to a common ancestor who was traditionally linked to a Vedic sage (ṛiṣhi). Patrilineal means that family identity and inheritance pass strictly through the male line.

Gotra often played an important role in regulating marriage. So a person's family line could shape whom that person married.

We have placed a person inside these groups. Next we follow one person through a whole life.

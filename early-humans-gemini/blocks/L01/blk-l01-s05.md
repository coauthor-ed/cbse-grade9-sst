# Scripts We Can Read, and One We Cannot

<!-- STEP id="seal" -->
## A Seal with Writing on It

Put a small carved square of stone beside the chipped one. It is about the size of a postage stamp.

A bull stands across most of it, with a vessel below its head, and a row of five signs runs along the top. It is about 4,500 years old, from the Sindhu-Sarasvati civilisation.

**This object carries writing, and nobody alive can read it.**

<!-- STEP id="signs" -->
## Five Signs

Look at that row of signs on its own, because that is the whole inscription. An inscription is writing cut or pressed into a hard surface. Most inscriptions in this script are about that short.

The script is called the Sindhu lipi, or the Harappan script. It has not been deciphered, which means that nobody has worked out how to read it.

<!-- STEP id="brahmi" -->
## A Younger Stone We Can Read

Now look at this sandstone railing from the stupa at Sanchi, in Madhya Pradesh. A line of Brahmi is cut across it.

Brahmi was used from about 400 BCE in southern India and the Ganga Valley, and Emperor Ashoka later formalised it in the third century BCE. This railing is about half the age of the seal, and scholars can read it.

<!-- STEP id="cuneiform" -->
## Two More Scripts We Can Read

This clay tablet from Mesopotamia is pressed all over with wedge-shaped marks, a script called cuneiform. It is from the same long period as the seal, and scholars have been able to read it for well over a century.

Egyptian hieroglyphs have been deciphered too. We will meet both scripts later in this chapter.

<!-- STEP id="bilingual" -->
## What Helped

Scholars who deciphered these scripts had help from the same kind of object: a text with a known script beside it. The same message appears at least twice, so a guess at one can be checked against the other.

Nothing of that kind has been found for the Sindhu lipi.

**The seal is not hard to read because it is worn, but because nothing can check a guess.**

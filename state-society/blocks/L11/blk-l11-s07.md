# Money Left with a Guild

<!-- STEP -->
## A gift of a cave

This is a source you can read for yourself. An inscription of the 2nd century CE in a cave at **Nāśhik** names the donor: **Uṣhavadāta**, son of Dinika and son-in-law of the Śhaka king Nahapāna.

He gave the cave to the **Saṁgha**, the community of Buddhist monks. In Lesson 2 a gaṇa or saṁgha was a republic; here the word means the community of monks.

<!-- STEP -->
## A deposit in two guilds

He also gave a perpetual endowment of **3000 kāhāpaṇas** (coins). A perpetual endowment is money placed so that its interest keeps paying for all time.

- 2000 went to one weavers' guild, at one pratika a month for each hundred.
- 1000 went to another weavers' guild, at three quarters of a paḍika a month for each hundred.

<!-- STEP -->
## The interest

The inscription gives monthly rates. Working them out for a year, taking one pratika or paḍika as one kāhāpaṇa, gives 12 for each hundred and 9 for each hundred. That is our arithmetic and not the inscription's wording.

So the guilds paid 240 + 90 = **330 kāhāpaṇas** a year. This served as cloth money and money for outside life for members of the Saṁgha "of any sect, and any origin" dwelling in the cave.

<!-- STEP -->
## Why trust a guild?

Guilds were obligated to pay a fixed rate of interest on these deposits. So they were more than associations of craftspeople: they were banking and credit institutions.

Why would a donor trust a guild with money for all time? Think about what you know: guilds had rules, courts and a steady trade.

<!-- STEP -->
## Other guilds, other work

Sources mention silk, cotton, wool and linen, and **Mathurā**, **Kāśhī** and **Kāmarūpa** were important textile centres.

Another kind of guild worked at Sanchi. An inscription on the southern gateway of the Great Stupa refers to the guild of ivory workers from **Vidiśhā**, who carved the stone sculptures there.

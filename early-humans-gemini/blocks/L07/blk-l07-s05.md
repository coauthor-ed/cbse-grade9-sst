# Two Crops, One Field

<!-- STEP id="verdict" -->
## What the Ground Shows

Here is what is certain. Early Harappan farmers at Kalibangan ploughed this field, and they ploughed it in two directions at right angles.

The book reads this as double crop cultivation, which means growing two crops on one piece of land. If you chose that answer, you read it the way the book does.

<!-- STEP id="evidence" -->
## Why Historians Read It This Way

The furrows do not say what was sown, and no Harappan farmer's record can be read. So "two crops" is an inference, a conclusion drawn from evidence.

The excavators compared the field with farming in the same area today. Farmers there still sow two crops across each other, mustard in wide rows and gram in close rows.

**The furrows are the evidence. The two crops are the best reading of them.**

<!-- STEP id="tradition" -->
## Rabi, Kharif and an Old Idea

The book also compares this field with Rabi and Kharif. Those are two crops from one field in two seasons: Kharif is sown with the monsoon and Rabi after it.

The crossing rows seem to show two crops in one season, so the two are not exactly the same practice. They share one idea, which is getting two crops from the same land. That idea is nearly 5,000 years old, and farmers in India still use it.

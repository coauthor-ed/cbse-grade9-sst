# Why Call It a Revolution?

<!-- STEP id="control" -->
## First Link: Control

The book says that people first became familiar with the seasons and with different food sources. Historians connect what followed farming as a chain, one link at a time. The chain is their explanation, and it did not run the same way in every region. The first link is control: people now decided which plants and animals were raised.

<!-- STEP id="surplus" -->
## Second Link: Surplus

In a good year, the fields gave more grain than a family could eat. The grain left over is called a **surplus**, and it can be stored for later.

<!-- STEP id="sedentism" -->
## Third Link: Staying in One Place

Stored food and fields that need care give a family reasons to stay all year. A camp becomes a village. The chart showed one exception: in West Asia, people settled before they farmed.

<!-- STEP id="specialists" -->
## Fourth Link: Other Kinds of Work

If a surplus can feed some people, they can spend their days on other work, such as pottery, weaving or metalwork. People who do one kind of skilled work are called specialists.

<!-- STEP id="village" -->
## Fifth Link: A Bigger Village

A village with stored grain, a potter, a weaver and a coppersmith is more than a group of farming families. The book says that these first villages laid the foundations for towns and cities.

<!-- STEP id="cost" -->
## Easier or Harder?

Was life better after farming? The book asks you this question, and there are real reasons on both sides.

- **Harder:** more hours of work, one bad harvest hurts everybody, and people ate fewer kinds of food.
- **Easier:** food could be stored for the lean months, and a family had a fixed home.

<!-- STEP id="verdict" -->
## Why "Revolution"?

The change took thousands of years, so it was not a sudden event. It is called a revolution because so much depended on it: towns, trade and writing all came later and all needed stored food.

**It was not fast, but it changed how people lived.**

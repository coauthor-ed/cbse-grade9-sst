# Most Votes Wins

<!-- STEP -->
## It Depends on the Rule

The answer depends on the country's rule. The way votes become seats is what makes one electoral system different from another.

We will use three imaginary countries: Haritbhumi, Ratnadweep and Swarnalok. In **Haritbhumi**, each voter votes for one candidate in their constituency, and the candidate with the most votes wins, even with less than 50 per cent. So there, Party A has won.

<!-- STEP -->
## Most Votes, or More Than Half?

This rule is called **First Past The Post (FPTP)**, or the **plurality** system. A plurality means more votes than anyone else. A **majority** means more than half of the votes.

Party A's 40 is a plurality but not a majority, because 60 of every 100 votes went to other parties. Under FPTP, that is enough.

<!-- STEP -->
## Twenty-Five Seats

Haritbhumi has 25 seats. The count differs from seat to seat. On this made-up map, the country's votes add up to A 40, B 35 and C 25 per cent, and A comes first in 15 seats.

So Party A wins **15 seats**, B wins 7 and C wins 3. Tap a constituency to see its count. **Party A has less than half of the votes, and it forms the government.**

<!-- STEP -->
## India Chose This Rule

The framers of our Constitution discussed the two most common electoral systems and chose FPTP. We elect members of the Lok Sabha and the Vidhan Sabha in this way.

**The other two countries use other rules, and we meet them next.**

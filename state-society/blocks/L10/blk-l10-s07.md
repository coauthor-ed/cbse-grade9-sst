# Rite and Song

## Fire, rain and dawn

Vedic deities had no rigid order of rank, and different hymns praise different deities as supreme. People called on them through prayers and sacrificial rituals (**yajñas**), with offerings most often made to the sacred fire.

Each deity was linked to part of nature, such as the sun, rain, fire or dawn. Early societies depended on nature to survive, so they saw it as sacred and powerful. Some of these practices continue today, such as the sun worship during Chhath and on Makar Sankranti.

## Those who left home

Remember the last two stages of life. Around the middle of the first millennium BCE, a clear trend of **renunciants** appeared: people who chose to live as ascetics and wanderers, rather than as householders.

Sources call them **parivrājaka** (wanderer), **bhikṣhu** (one who lives by begging alms) or **śhramaṇa** (one who strives). Gautama Buddha and Mahāvīra were influential thinkers of this period.

The idea was not new, because vānaprastha and saṁnyāsa already carried it in the Vedic tradition.

## A direct path

Over time, worship moved towards personal deities such as **Viṣhṇu**, **Śhiva** and **Śhakti**. One practice that grew from this was **Bhakti**.

Bhakti offered a direct connection with the gods without the elaborate rituals prescribed in the Vedas. It was accessible to all, regardless of class or gender, and early references to it appear in texts such as the Mahābhārata.

<!-- STEP -->
## Twelve and sixty-three

The organised, widespread Bhakti tradition gained prominence in the 6th-century Tamil region through two groups of saint-poets.

- The twelve **Āḻvārs** composed hymns in praise of Lord Viṣhṇu.
- The sixty-three **Nāyanmārs**, the Śhaiva saint-poets, composed hymns in praise of Lord Śhiva.

Together they created a large body of Tamil Bhakti literature.

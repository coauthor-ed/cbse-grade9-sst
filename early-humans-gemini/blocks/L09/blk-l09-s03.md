# Wedges

<!-- STEP id="tablet" -->
## Reed Into Clay

From about 3200 BCE, Sumerian scribes pressed a sharp, wedge-shaped reed into the damp surface of a clay tablet. A reed pressed into clay leaves a neat wedge. A reed dragged through clay tears it, so the marks came out straight and not curved.

<!-- STEP id="impressions" -->
## The Name Fits the Shape

The word cuneiform comes from the Latin *cuneus*, which means wedge. So the name simply says "wedge-shaped writing".

<!-- STEP id="closeup" -->
## One Sign, Many Wedges

Each sign is built from several wedges pressed side by side. Cuneiform had hundreds of these signs, so a scribe had a lot to learn.

<!-- STEP id="press" -->
## Make One Sign

A scribe pressed the reed, lifted it, turned it and pressed again. Try it here: press the reed four times to make the sign that meant sky, and also god.

<!-- STEP id="lists" -->
## Lists First

Numbers were easy to make: a straight wedge for 1 and a corner mark for 10. Tablets kept records of farming and of crafts, listing the work of potters, seal cutters, shipbuilders, carpenters and farm workers.

<!-- STEP id="verdict" -->
## From Lists to Stories

By 3000 BCE, cuneiform was used all over Mesopotamia by city-states that spoke different languages, and scribes had a high status, like priests and priestesses. Later tablets hold myths, hymns, law codes and lessons as well.

**The oldest tablets are mostly lists. Stories and laws came later, in the same signs.**

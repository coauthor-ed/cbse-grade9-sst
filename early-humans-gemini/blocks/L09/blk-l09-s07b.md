# One Law, Three Prices

<!-- STEP id="scope" -->
## Written Down for All to See

From outside the book: the Code of Hammurabi ran to 282 laws, carved into a 2.25-metre stele of black basalt and set up in public. It went far beyond crime, into the ordinary business of the empire:

- trade and debt
- wages and inheritance
- marriage and injury

<!-- STEP id="importance" -->
## Why It Mattered

Hammurabi's empire had taken in many older city-states, and each had its own courts and customs. The Code set out one written law for all of them, though how closely each town followed it is still debated.

Later law-makers took up that idea: one law, written down, for a whole state. But one law did not mean one price.

<!-- STEP id="talion" -->
## Same Offence, Three Prices

Two free citizens of high rank, one offence: destroy the other's eye, and the law took your own eye in return. This is "an eye for an eye". Change who was hurt, and the price changed too:

- a free citizen's eye: the offender's own eye
- a commoner's eye: a fine in silver
- a slave's eye: half the slave's market value, paid to the owner, not to the slave

<!-- STEP id="verdict" -->
## What the Code Said About Itself

Hammurabi's own prologue, the opening of the Code, gave its purpose: "so that the strong might not oppress the weak."

**The penalties were graded by the same rank the prologue promised to guard against.**

Was the Code fair to all sections of society? It protected the weak more than having no written law. It also gave them less than it gave the strong. Which of these two matters more to you, and why?

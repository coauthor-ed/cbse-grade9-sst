# What Holds Things Up

<!-- STEP -->
## The root of the word

The word **dharma** comes from the Sanskrit root **dhri**, which is also the root of **dharati**, the earth. The earth holds up everything that stands on it, and this picture helps you remember the root.

Bhishma's line gives the same sense: dharma is "that which upholds beings".

<!-- STEP -->
## The law of a thing

The saying about iron and wood uses dharma in the sense of the **law of existence**: the way a thing is. Iron sinks and wood floats because of what each one is, and no order from anyone changes that.

## Duties that go with a role

In this sense dharma does not mean religion. It refers to **duty, obligation, righteousness and moral conduct**, and people are expected to perform their duties according to their roles in society.

Tap a role to see one duty that goes with it. In Buddhism, the Pāli word **dhamma** expresses similar ethical principles.

## Satya and ṛita

The Vedas describe one supreme consciousness as **satya** (real, true) and **ṛita** (right).

The Ṛig Veda describes ṛita as an all-pervading cosmic order. It regulates the forces of nature and upholds moral values, so it stands for harmony and balance in nature and in human society.

<!-- STEP -->
## Samatva

**Samatva** is the principle of sameness: all bodies are made of the same matter, and all forms of consciousness are manifestations of one supreme consciousness.

The Mahābhārata has characters from all varṇas who uphold this principle. The epics often critique discrimination, and they present the ideal of a good person and ruler "as one who works for the welfare of all beings."
The **Bhagavada Gītā** emphasises knowledge, faith, action, virtue and an ethical way of life.
